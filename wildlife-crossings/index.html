Santa Barbara County · Caltrans District 5

Where should wildlife cross Santa Barbara County's highways?

Community scientists have logged hundreds of animals killed on the county's roads. Combined with Caltrans drainage inventories, traffic, terrain and statewide connectivity maps, those records point to a short list of highway stretches where crossings would do the most good, and to the kind of crossing each one needs.

Garrett Craig · Analysis code and data: github.com/garrettgcraig/CA_crossings

Context

A new law asks Caltrans to plan for wildlife

On September 30, 2026, Governor Newsom signed SB 1250 (Cortese). It directs Caltrans to fold wildlife connectivity into routine highway planning: wildlife crossings, culverts, habitat corridors and fencing now belong in the department's asset management plan, and Caltrans must keep an inventory of connectivity needs jointly with the California Department of Fish and Wildlife.

That inventory needs evidence about where animals actually try to cross. Most of the existing structures under Santa Barbara County's highways are drainage culverts built to move water, and most are far too small or too enclosed for anything larger than a skunk. Replacing them on the usual maintenance cycle is the cheapest moment to make them work for wildlife, if the right ones are identified in time.

This analysis scores every half-kilometer of state highway in the county, ranks the stretches where a crossing would help most, and matches each to a practical solution: retrofitting a bridge or culvert that is already there, upsizing a culvert at replacement, or building a new underpass or overpass where the terrain allows.

Room to Roam roadkill survey

Volunteers with ForestWatch recorded dead and live animals along county roads through a Survey123 form starting in July 2025. This analysis uses the records collected in 2025; after cleaning, 187 of them fall within 10 m of a road in the county.

iNaturalist

421 more road-killed animals, from all years through 2026, come from three iNaturalist projects, each a photo record with a location accurate to 100 m or better. Many of their photos appear on the map below.

Already underway at Gaviota Pass

Caltrans received $8 million to retrofit a culvert and add about 2.5 miles of wildlife fencing on US 101 at Gaviota Pass, a CDFW priority barrier. It serves here as a check: a sound ranking should flag it even where roadkill records are sparse.

Results

State Route 154 tops the list, and Gaviota Pass checks out

The highest-need stretches cluster on SR 154 between San Marcos Pass and Lake Cachuma (postmiles 12 to 21), which holds three of the five highest-ranked zones. Continuous oak woodland and chaparral meet the road on both sides, CDFW already lists the route as a movement barrier (W165), and more roadkill records fall there than in any other zone. Its existing culverts are tall enough for bobcats, foxes and raccoons, so fencing that funnels animals to them is the quickest gain.

US 101 at Gaviota Pass ranks in the top tier too: 10 of its 21 segments land in the top decile (median: 89th percentile), carried mostly by habitat and connectivity because only four roadkill records fall there. That matches the site Caltrans and CDFW chose independently.

Other strong candidates are US 101 between Los Olivos and Orcutt (postmiles 62 to 79, five zones), where more than 30,000 vehicles a day pass through oak woodland and rangeland, and SR 1 north of Lompoc (postmiles 23 to 33), where the highway runs through cuts suited to overpasses on two CDFW-listed barriers (W048, W049). How stable is the ranking?

Under six alternative weightings of the score, 69% to 89% of the top-decile segments stay in the top decile. A separate ranking that adds roadside barriers for the surveyed routes agrees closely with the main one (rank correlation 0.85), and SR 154 and Gaviota Pass lead both.

Methods

What went into the ranking

Every state highway in the county was cut into 953 segments of about 500 m. Each segment carries everything known about that stretch of road and its surroundings, and the ranking combines those measures into a single need score. The full analysis is a set of reproducible R and Quarto notebooks in the project repository.

  1. Clean the roadkill

    Merge the survey's two form versions with iNaturalist, assign species to groups by the size of crossing they'd use, and keep dead animals within 10 m of a road.

  2. Inventory the structures

    Join Caltrans' 19,691 culvert records to their end treatments, keep the 633 county culverts open at both ends under the highway, and size them for wildlife by height and openness (area ÷ length).

  3. Describe each segment

    Traffic and lanes from FHWA's 2024 HPMS, speed limits from HPMS and OpenStreetMap, curvature and grade from the centerline, embankment and side slopes from 10 m USGS elevation, and habitat and protected land on each side.

  4. Find roadkill hotspots

    Count records within 1 km of each segment and compare with 999 random scatterings of the same records along the network.

  5. Score need

    Weighted percentile ranks of six components, discounted where steep terrain keeps animals off the road (except where a creek channel funnels them to a structure). Each component is described below.

  6. Match a solution

    Choose retrofit, upsize, underpass or overpass from what already crosses the road and whether it sits on fill or in a cut, then merge adjacent top segments into zones.

Caveats

  • Survey effort is treated as equal across roads, though volunteers drive some routes far more than others. Only the survey's 2025 records are used; iNaturalist records from every year are included.
  • Roadside barriers were mapped from Street View on parts of US 101, SR 154, SR 246 and SR 1 only; they shape recommendations and a separate ranking, not the main score.
  • The collision component targets nighttime conditions because wildlife-vehicle collisions are concentrated after dark (Cunningham et al. 2022). The night stopping-sight measure uses standard assumptions (70 m low-beam reach, 6 m roadside clearance) rather than measured sight lines.
  • Each candidate zone needs a site visit before design: what the data shows as a culvert or bridge may differ on the ground.

What goes into the need score

Each component is converted to a percentile rank across the county's 953 highway segments, so 80 means a segment scores higher than 80% of segments on that measure. The weighted percentiles add up to a score from 0 to 100, which terrain access can reduce. Select any highway segment on the map, or open an example, to see its breakdown.

Roadkill hotspots

25%
Measures
Dead animals within 1 km of the segment, counting groups that use crossings (mammals, reptiles and amphibians; birds and unidentified animals left out).
How it's scored
Monte Carlo hotspot test: the same records are scattered at random along the network 999 times. A segment whose count beats the 95th percentile of those scatterings is a hotspot, and its score is how far it exceeds that line. Other segments score by raw count, below every hotspot.
Source
Room to Roam survey (2025 records) and iNaturalist (all years).
Why it matters
The most direct evidence of where animals try to cross and fail. Its weakness is effort: places people drive and report more look worse.

Connectivity priority

25%
Measures
How important the landscape around the road is for wildlife movement statewide.
How it's scored
The larger of the CDFW connectivity rank (1–5, divided by 5) and a CDFW priority-barrier score (Top Priority 1.0, Priority 0.75, Barrier 0.5), plus 0.1 inside a big-game priority area, capped at 1.
Source
CDFW Areas of Conservation Emphasis terrestrial connectivity (ds2734), priority wildlife movement barriers (ds2867) and big-game priority areas (ds2889).
Why it matters
Independent of anyone's survey effort, so it catches important corridors where few people report roadkill, like Gaviota Pass.

Habitat on both sides

20%
Measures
Share of natural land cover in a 300 m strip on each side of the road, taking the weaker side.
How it's scored
Tree, shrub, grassland, bare ground and wetland count as natural; cropland, buildings and open water do not. The lower of the two sides is ranked.
Source
ESA WorldCover 2021 (10 m).
Why it matters
A crossing only helps where there is habitat at both ends. Using the weaker side keeps a road beside a subdivision from scoring well because of the hills opposite.

Protected land

10%
Measures
Share of protected land in the same 300 m strips, weaker side.
How it's scored
Ranked like habitat. Most segments have no protected land on at least one side, and tied segments share a rank, so a value of zero still scores about the 45th percentile.
Source
California Protected Areas Database (CPAD 2024a).
Why it matters
A crossing is a decades-long investment. Protected land at both ends means the habitat it connects is likely to stay.

Traffic volume

10%
Measures
Average annual daily traffic (AADT).
How it's scored
Ranked on the logarithm of AADT, so going from 2,000 to 20,000 vehicles a day counts as much as 20,000 to 200,000.
Source
FHWA Highway Performance Monitoring System 2024.
Why it matters
More traffic makes a road a stronger barrier and raises collision risk, though the effect levels off: a road busy enough to turn most animals back can't get much worse.

Night stopping risk

10%
Measures
How much farther a driver needs to stop at the speed limit than they can see at night.
How it's scored
AASHTO stopping distance at the segment's speed (2.5 s reaction, 3.4 m/s² braking) minus the visible distance, which is the shorter of low-beam reach (about 70 m for a dark, animal-sized object) and the sight line around curves. Averaged along the segment.
Source
Speed limits from HPMS and OpenStreetMap (via Overture Maps); curvature from the Caltrans centerline.
Why it matters
Wildlife-vehicle collisions are concentrated after dark, when headlights limit warning. Roadkill per km rises from 0.34 to 0.51 between the lowest and highest thirds of this measure.

Terrain access

× 0.5–1
Measures
Share of the steeper roadside strip (30–150 m from the road) with slopes over 35°.
How it's scored
The weighted sum is multiplied by 0.5 + 0.5 × (1 − steep share), so a segment walled in by cliffs keeps at most half its score. The discount is waived where a waterway bridge or a culvert at least 0.9 m tall carries a drainage under the road.
Source
USGS 3DEP 1/3 arc-second elevation (about 10 m).
Why it matters
Animals rarely climb cliffs to reach a road, but canyon walls funnel them along creeks to the structures beneath it, as at Gaviota Pass.

Do the components overlap? Only modestly. Across all segments, the strongest rank correlation is between connectivity and habitat (0.43), since CDFW's connectivity ranks draw partly on natural land cover, so natural, well-connected land counts for a bit more than its nominal weight. Protected land looks independent overall (0.18 with connectivity), but 90% of segments have none on at least one side; among the 98 segments with protected land on both sides, it tracks connectivity at 0.49. No pair comes near the 0.7 or so where two measures would duplicate each other.
