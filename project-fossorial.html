Wildlife Use of Artificial Refuges at a Restoration Site

Ecological Restoration Camera Traps Wildlife

Do rubble-filled refuges help burrowing animals recolonize a site that grading left bare?

  • Read More

Overview

Large restoration projects that regrade a site also wipe out its burrows, the below-ground habitat built up over years by ground squirrels and other ecosystem engineers. At North Campus Open Space, a 40-hectare former golf course that UC Santa Barbara's Cheadle Center for Biodiversity and Ecological Restoration regraded in 2017, crews built artificial refuges from salvaged concrete rubble set into excavated holes, alongside logs and boulders.

This study used camera traps in 2021 to compare how wildlife used the refuges versus logs and boulders across restored grassland, coastal scrub, and salt marsh.

My role

I'm second author on the manuscript, with Alistair Dobson (lead), Francis Joyce, and Lisa Stratton of the Cheadle Center. Alistair designed and ran the field study. I analyzed the data under Francis Joyce's guidance and co-wrote the manuscript with Alistair.

What I did

  • Built the analysis in R: negative binomial regression of wildlife detections by feature type, habitat type, and trail adjacency, with camera hours as an offset for sampling effort
  • Checked model fit with simulation-based residual diagnostics (DHARMa) and tested for spatial autocorrelation with Moran's I (spdep)
  • Compared candidate models with AICc and Akaike weights and model-averaged the competitive set (MuMIn)
  • Standardized detections across two camera-trap seasons into hourly presence records and detection rates per camera day for species-level comparisons
  • Co-wrote and edited the manuscript

Status

The manuscript is under peer review. I'll share results here once it's published.
